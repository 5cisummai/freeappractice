Falling behind in an AP class can make every assignment feel like it depends on five things you missed. It is tempting to start at chapter one and promise yourself you'll catch up over a weekend. That plan usually competes with the work your class is still assigning.

Start with the next thing you need to understand, and work backward only as far as necessary.

## Figure out what's actually missing

Take ten minutes to list your current assignments, the next quiz or test, and any missing work that can still be submitted. Check your class portal or ask your teacher about deadlines and credit. A missing assignment and an unfamiliar concept are different problems, even if they came from the same week.

Next, choose one current topic and ask: “What earlier idea is stopping me from understanding this?” In calculus, trouble with derivatives might come from algebra or functions. In history, a confusing argument might come from not knowing the events and chronology behind it. Write down the specific gap rather than putting the whole unit on your list.

## Decide what needs attention first

Your list may be longer than the time you have. Sort it by what happens if you leave something alone for another week.

Start with work that has a deadline you can still meet. Then look at the concepts your class is using right now. Finally, note older material that isn't holding up the current unit but will eventually need review. You can come back to that third list when the immediate pressure has eased.

For example, say you've missed two AP Biology assignments, the class has moved on to cellular respiration, and there's a quiz on Friday. You might:

- Ask whether the two assignments can still earn credit and how long they are likely to take.
- Review the basics of enzymes if the current lesson isn't making sense without them.
- Leave a detailed review of an earlier, unrelated topic for a later session.

Your teacher may suggest a different order, especially if an assignment is designed to teach a prerequisite. The point of the list is to make a choice, not to create one more task to complete perfectly.

## Protect time for current work

Try dividing your available study time between keeping up and catching up. For instance, if you have 45 minutes, spend 25 on the current assignment and 20 on a prerequisite you identified. Adjust that split when a deadline is close or a foundation needs more attention.

The goal is to stop the gap from growing while you repair it. Some days the current assignment will take the whole session. That doesn't mean the plan failed; it means you can return to the older topic next time.

## Use a short feedback loop

For each gap, follow a simple sequence:

1. Review one explanation in your notes, textbook, or a resource your teacher recommends.
2. Close it and explain the idea or work a small example on your own.
3. Try a few questions and check your reasoning.
4. Write down what still doesn't make sense.

If you're stuck after a reasonable attempt, bring that last note to your teacher, a classmate, or a tutor. “I don't understand this unit” is hard to answer. “I can find the slope, but I don't know what it represents in this graph” gives someone a place to start.

## What catching up can look like in different subjects

The same plan works across classes, but the kind of practice you need will change.

### Math and science: find the first step you can't do

Take a problem similar to one assigned in class and try it without looking at the solution. When you get stuck, identify the first step you couldn't make. If you can't simplify the algebra, watching a long video about the later calculus concept may not solve the immediate problem.

Review that step, do two or three short examples, and then return to the original problem. If you can follow a worked solution but can't start a new problem, try explaining why each step is there. Ask your teacher to check your setup before you spend half an hour repeating the same mistake.

In a lab-based class, the missing piece may be interpretation rather than calculation. Practice describing what a graph shows, then explain how the evidence relates to the claim. Those are separate skills, and knowing which one is difficult makes it easier to get help.

### History and social science: rebuild the sequence

If a reading assumes you know events you missed, make a rough timeline before trying to memorize every name and date. Include a few major events, what led to them, and what changed afterward. Keep your textbook or class notes nearby so you can check your version.

Then return to the current lesson and ask how its argument fits into that sequence. For a source-based question, identify who created the source, when, and what claim it makes before deciding how it supports an answer. A timeline won't do the analysis for you, but it can make the documents less disorienting.

### English and other writing-heavy classes: work with one response

If you have several readings or essays to catch up on, ask your teacher which one will be used in the next discussion or assignment. Read that first. As you go, note the author's main claim, a few passages that support it, and questions you want to bring to class.

For writing practice, choose one paragraph from an earlier assignment. Check whether it makes a clear point, uses relevant evidence, and explains how that evidence supports the point. Revise that paragraph before trying to rewrite an entire essay. If you have teacher feedback, use it to decide what to focus on.

## Ask for help with a specific starting point

You don't need to wait until you've tried every resource on your own. A short conversation can save you from spending a week studying the wrong material. You could send your teacher a message like this:

> Hi, I've fallen behind on the last two units and I'm having trouble following the current work. I've reviewed my notes and tried the first few problems, but I'm stuck on how to set them up. Which topic or assignment should I prioritize before the next quiz? Could we go over one example?

Bring the list you made and an example of what you've tried. If office hours conflict with another commitment, ask whether there's another time to meet or a resource your teacher recommends. A classmate can also talk through notes you missed, though it's still worth checking assignment and grading questions with your teacher.

## Make the next week realistic

Put two or three catch-up sessions on your calendar, with a specific topic for each. Leave room for other classes, activities, and sleep. A 20-minute session you can repeat is more useful than a four-hour plan you can't fit in.

Here's an example for a student with a quiz on Friday and about 30 minutes available on three evenings:

- **Monday:** List the quiz topics, try a few problems, and pick the one prerequisite causing the most trouble.
- **Wednesday:** Review that prerequisite, work through a fresh example, and finish the most urgent current assignment.
- **Thursday:** Try a short mixed set without notes. Review the questions you miss and write down anything to ask before the quiz.

This isn't a guarantee that every gap will disappear by Friday. It gives you a way to use limited time and notice what still needs attention. If the quiz covers a lot of material or you have more than one class to recover, tell your teacher early rather than quietly trying to do everything alone.

At the end of the week, check whether you can follow the current lesson more easily and complete a few problems without looking at an example. If not, narrow the gap further or ask for help choosing what to prioritize. You don't have to recover every missed detail before you can make progress.

## After the immediate deadline

Once the quiz or assignment is over, don't throw away your list. Cross off what you've handled and pick one older topic to revisit in a later session. Your quiz feedback can help: look for a recurring misunderstanding rather than treating every lost point as a separate emergency.

If your workload still isn't manageable after a week or two, talk with your teacher or counselor about the bigger picture. They can help you understand your options and decide which support would be useful. Catching up is easier when the plan accounts for everything else you have to do.
