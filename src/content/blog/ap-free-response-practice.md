A blank page can make an AP free-response question feel much bigger than it is. You have to understand the prompt, decide what evidence or work to include, and get it down clearly. Practicing those steps helps, but you don't have to write a complete response every time you study.

The useful part is getting feedback on a specific skill. Some days that means writing a full answer. Other days, a ten-minute plan and a careful look at the scoring guidelines will show you what to work on.

## Read the task before you start writing

Underline the action words and mark each part of the prompt. “Identify,” “describe,” and “explain” ask for different levels of response. Check the course's scoring guidelines for what earns credit, since expectations vary by subject and question type.

For example, if a prompt asks you to identify a trend and explain it using data, naming the trend alone leaves part of the task unanswered. You need to connect evidence from the data to your explanation.

Before writing, make a quick list of the parts you need to answer. For a multipart science question, label them (a), (b), and (c). For an essay, jot down your main claim and the evidence you might use. A brief plan can prevent a long, polished answer to only half the question.

## Pick one kind of practice

Match the exercise to the time you have and the skill that needs work:

- **10 minutes:** Read a prompt, outline your answer, and compare your plan with the scoring guidelines. Check whether you included every part.
- **20 minutes:** Write one section or a short response, then review it against the guidelines. Revise the part that lacks evidence or explanation.
- **A longer session:** Complete a full question under the time conditions appropriate to your exam. Leave time afterward to score and review it.

Planning alone won't replace writing practice. If timing or expressing your reasoning is difficult, make room for full responses too. The shorter sessions are useful between those attempts.

## Score what you wrote, not what you meant

Use official scoring guidelines and sample responses when available. Read your answer as someone who cannot see your notes or hear your thoughts. Did you actually state the claim? Did you show the calculation or connect the evidence to the conclusion when the prompt required it?

Mark the exact spot where you earned a point or where an answer needs more. If you're unsure, ask a teacher to look at it. Self-scoring gets easier with practice, but it isn't always obvious whether a response meets a rubric.

Then rewrite one weak portion. Keep the original next to it so you can see the difference. For example, replace “the value increased” with a description of the relevant change in the data, followed by an explanation that answers the prompt. The revision matters more than copying the sample answer word for word.

## Build toward exam conditions

Early on, it is reasonable to use notes and take your time. As you become more comfortable, try questions without notes and use the timing for your particular exam. Practice moving on when you're stuck, then return if time remains.

After each response, choose one thing to carry into the next attempt: label every part, show a calculation, use specific evidence, or explain the connection more clearly. Small, specific adjustments make the next blank page easier to start.
