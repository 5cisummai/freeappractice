Getting a question wrong can be useful, but only if you spend a little time with it afterward. Clicking through the explanation, thinking “that makes sense,” and moving on is easy. A week later, the same kind of question can still catch you.

You don't need a detailed spreadsheet or a page of notes for every mistake. You need to know why your answer missed the mark and what to do differently next time.

## Start before you read the explanation

Look at the question again and commit to what you think went wrong. Did you forget a concept? Misread what the question asked? Set up the calculation correctly but make an arithmetic error?

Then read the explanation for both the correct answer and your choice. If your choice still seems reasonable, find the specific detail that makes it less suitable. In a history question, that might be a date outside the period in the prompt. In a science question, it might be a conclusion the data doesn't actually support.

## Name the mistake precisely

“I'm bad at chemistry” isn't a study plan. “I confused intermolecular forces with bonds within a molecule” gives you something you can fix.

Most misses fall into a few useful categories:

- **Content gap:** You didn't know or couldn't recall the idea. Revisit that concept, then explain it in your own words.
- **Application gap:** You knew the idea but didn't recognize how it applied. Work through another example and compare the reasoning.
- **Reading error:** You missed a qualifier, mixed up what a graph showed, or answered a different question. Go back to the words or evidence that should have guided you.
- **Process error:** Your approach was sound, but a sign, calculation, or step went wrong. Redo the problem and find the first incorrect step.

Sometimes more than one applies. That's fine. Pick the one that would make the biggest difference if you saw a similar question tomorrow.

## Keep a small correction log

For questions worth revisiting, write three short lines:

1. **Topic:** What was this testing?
2. **Why I missed it:** What did I misunderstand or overlook?
3. **Next time:** What will I check or do differently?

For example: “Confidence intervals. I thought a 95% interval means there's a 95% probability this fixed interval contains the true parameter. Review what the confidence level says about the method over repeated samples.”

You don't need to log every typo. Look for mistakes that reveal a misunderstanding or keep showing up. If your log becomes longer than the practice session, make it smaller.

## Come back without the answer in front of you

After a day or two, cover your notes and try a fresh question on the same idea. Explain why the correct answer works before checking. If you miss it again, go back to the underlying lesson or ask your teacher for help. If you get it right, mix that topic into later practice instead of drilling it indefinitely.

A good review session should leave you with a few clear things to work on, not a record of everything you've ever missed. Try a short set on [Free AP Practice](/subjects), then spend as much attention on the questions that surprised you as on the score at the end.
